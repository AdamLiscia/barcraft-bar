I'm Adam Liscia. I tend bar at Bedford Post Tavern, and on my nights off I work private events across Westchester and Fairfield County. This is the part of the site where I tell you why that's worth what I charge.

The room I work in

Bedford Post Tavern is a Relais & Châteaux property. That tells you the tier. What it doesn't tell you is what working in a bar like that actually does to a bartender.

It's a program that treats a cocktail as a process, not a performance. Every drink has an order of operations worked out in advance — what gets built first, which motion happens while the shaker is going, when the glass comes off the ice. Nothing is free-poured; every ingredient goes through a jigger. Every syrup is weighed on a scale rather than eyeballed, so the batch made in March tastes like the one made in November. It is a level of deliberateness most people have never seen at a bar.

Your backyard is not that bar, and I'm not going to pretend it is. There's no glass fridge on your patio, and I'm not going to make eight people wait while I jigger a vodka soda. What travels isn't the equipment — it's knowing exactly what a drink is supposed to taste like, and having built it correctly enough thousands of times to land there without stopping to measure. Years of not free-pouring are what buy you the right to free-pour and still be correct.

I lead the batch program at the Tavern — the cocktails we build in volume ahead of service. When a hundred drinks have to taste like the one that got approved, that's my job. That part transfers to your party completely, and it's the single skill that makes fifty guests work with one bartender and no line.

How I bartend

An event isn't a restaurant shift. Here's what carries over.

Batch what should be batched

The reason your party can have one bartender and no line is that most of the work happened yesterday. A batched cocktail isn't a punch bowl — it's the drink built to spec at volume, diluted to exactly where a shaken drink lands, and chilled so it pours in four seconds instead of ninety. Done right, no one can tell. Done wrong, everyone can.

Fast hands, right ratios

At a party I'm not going to jigger a vodka soda while eight people wait — I'll pour it, and it'll be right. That's what the day job buys. Where the ratio actually decides the drink — anything shaken, anything batched, anything with citrus in it — it gets measured, every time.

Work clean, work quiet

A good bar is boring to watch. Ice where it belongs, garnish cut ahead, tools in the same place all night, nothing scrambling in front of guests. Whatever chaos there is happens before the doors open — and your kitchen gets left the way I found it.

Half the job isn't the drink

People come to a bar to talk to somebody. Twelve years in dining rooms teaches you to read a room — who's had enough, who's standing alone, who wants to hear what's in the glass and who just wants the glass. At a private party that's worth more than any technique.

I also built the app

BarCraft is a cocktail app I wrote for working bartenders — look up a spec, save your own, scale it for batching with the dilution handled. The math in it is the math I use at work. I have a degree in mathematics from Stony Brook, which is a strange thing to bring to a bar right up until you're scaling a recipe to a hundred and forty servings and the dilution has to land exactly right.

Mostly it means that when I tell you three cocktails will be batched and ready before your first guest arrives, that's an arithmetic problem I've already solved, not a promise I'm hoping to keep.
